I read Barry Schwartz's *The Paradox of Choice* about a year ago or so. I would know when, if I had remembered to log onto my goodreads account and taken a record of it, but my success rate there is under 50 percent. There's something deeply ironic about needing to sign into a website to remember when you read a book

There's a certain set of books where the author has an opinion, and the entire novel is them defending it, writing as if it is the Thing That Is Wrong (or, rather, the Thing That Will Fix you/your workplace/society/etc). For example, Marie Kondo's bit is that having a clean house will fix you, and the effective mechanism to achive this is throwing away all your belongings and storing your clothes vertically in drawers. The clothes bit makes more sense when you learn she's 4'8". 

In *The Paradox of Choice*, that author's bit is that the act of choosing between similar options, the process of optimizing to the utility/relevancy/functionality/happiness the decision will bring to your life, makes you less happier overall. And (the "The Thing That Is Wrong") in modern American society, there's too many goddamn options, and it's the reason we're all so fucking depressed.

> Note that this was published [A few months before I was born](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2004/03/01/select-all) back in a time where I'm told people thought the world was becoming a better place. At least for white americans, which is really what the "we" refers to here

While I think it's lower on the list of priorities for society, I've been pretty sold on this. Walk into any grocery story to buy eggs and you'll be forced to optimize on size, cost, quantity, and how abused the chickens were. In my local grocery store there are (N) options. 

When you have to optimize multiple parameters at once, there is no objectively correct answer. Not only that, but, you'll always feel as though you compromised. In my circles I feel like people get very personal about it as well, but on an intellectual level instead of a brand identification level. Whereas typical rich american status symbols tend to be how nice your SUV looks, in my circles you will be judged on your ability to make decisions in good taste, even irrelevant ones. People will hear about a problem and internally start thinking about the best solution, questioning you if you disagree, often not intentionally but in what feels like an attempt to demonstrate that yes, they are good at thinking, decision making, having the Best opinion, having good (better) taste.

Modern dating apps have the same problem. You have so many options. There are the people who meet all of your "requirements", but they're too hot to match with you. Everyone else has some number of indicators, either incompatability or generic red flags, that they won't be a good match. With all of the options, you feel as though you can choose between, ie, people who look like they go outside. And people who don't smoke. But you can't have both, and you'll be sad about it.
